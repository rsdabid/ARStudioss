# Governing AI in HR — Restructured Script (Motion Graphics Version)

**Source:** https://youtu.be/ds_blTN5YAU (transcript 0:00–6:27; the transcript we received ends mid-section)
**Target length:** ~5:30 at a measured ~150 wpm (original transcript covers ~6:30 and isn't finished)
**Voice:** Keep the original narrator's tone: calm, British-leaning, consultant style. Shorter sentences, same authority.

---

## Why the original loses people

| Problem | Where | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Slow hook. It takes ~40s to say what the video is about. | 0:00–1:03 | Cold open on three concrete disaster scenarios, then promise a clear payoff. |
| A series recap ("Throughout this September series…") right after the hook | 0:38–1:00 | Cut it. If it's needed, use an on-screen lower-third: *Part 4 of 4*. |
| No signposting. Viewers never know how many points there are or where they are. | Whole video | Three numbered rules plus a persistent progress tracker on screen. |
| Abstract nouns and hedging ("may", "should", "consequences", "arrangements"), with no concrete images | Whole video | Rewritten in plain, active language. Every point has a visual metaphor. |
| Broken section titles read aloud ("AI governance needs to ownership must survive shared responsibility") | 1:13 | Clean rule titles. |
| Repetition ("owner identifiable" is said 3 ways) | 2:50–3:13 | Merged into one "6-month test" moment. |
| Long lists spoken as run-on sentences | 3:18, 4:06, 5:42 | Turned into checklists and question cards that build on screen. |
| No ending or recap | — | 3-rule recap + callback to the opening. |

## SCENE 0 — Cold open (0:00–0:25)

**VO:**
> Every organisation using AI in HR will end up with rules for it.
> The only question is: what forces them to write those rules?
>
> Maybe a rejected candidate challenges a decision.
> Maybe an employee pastes confidential data into a public chatbot.
> Or maybe a supplier switches on an AI feature nobody knew existed.
>
> By then, you're writing policy to clean up a mess, not to prevent one.

**ON SCREEN:**
- An empty rulebook sits centre frame.
- Three alert cards slam in one by one, timed to the VO:
  1. Candidate icon + "DECISION CHALLENGED" stamp
  2. Employee typing → document flies into a chat bubble → red "CONFIDENTIAL" tag
  3. A settings toggle flips to ON by itself → "Who turned this on?"
- The rulebook slams shut and gets stamped **REACTIVE** in red.

## SCENE 2 — Rule 1: One tool, one owner (0:40–2:05)

**VO:**
> Rule one: every AI tool needs an owner. Not a committee. A person.

**ON SCREEN:** Title card: **RULE 1 — ONE TOOL, ONE OWNER**

**VO:**
> AI in HR touches a lot of teams.
> IT handles security and integration. Legal and risk handle obligations.
> Procurement handles suppliers and contracts. Data protection handles personal information.
> And HR understands the real impact on candidates, employees and managers.
>
> Every team owns a piece. Which means nobody owns the outcome.

**ON SCREEN:**
- Hub diagram: an "AI TOOL" node in the centre with five department icons orbiting it (IT, Legal, Procurement, Data Protection, HR).
- Each department grabs one puzzle piece and pulls it away.
- The centre is left as an empty slot labelled **OUTCOME: ???**

**VO:**
> A governance committee can provide oversight, but it can't be accountable for every single use.
> So each significant tool needs a named owner who can answer three questions:
> Why does this tool exist?
> How is it actually being used?
> And what would tell us something's gone wrong?

**ON SCREEN:**
- An ID badge drops into the empty slot: **OWNER — [Name]**
- A 3-item checklist ticks in beside it, one line per question.

**VO:**
> That owner doesn't need to be technical.
> They need enough understanding to ask good questions, and enough authority to act on the answers.
> They should know who reviews performance, who handles concerns, and who can hit pause.

**ON SCREEN:**
- The badge expands to show three contacts: Reviews performance · Handles concerns · Can pause
- A large ⏸ button pulses beside "Can pause".

**VO:**
> Leadership has a job here too.
> Executives set how far the organisation is willing to go with AI, and where the boundaries sit, especially across countries and business units.
> Push every hard question downwards, and managers end up accountable for problems they have no power to fix.

**ON SCREEN:**
- Org pyramid. Exec at the top draws a clear boundary line (good version).
- Rewind: "Hard question" boxes fall from the top and pile onto a manager icon at the bottom.
  Labels: **Accountability ✓ · Authority ✗**

**VO:**
> Here's the real test.
> Six months after launch, the project team has moved on and the tool is just part of normal work.
> Can anyone still tell you who's responsible?
> If not, your governance didn't survive contact with reality.

**ON SCREEN:**
- Calendar pages flip: **+6 MONTHS**
- The project team icons walk out of frame.
- A spotlight lands on the owner badge, which flickers to **???**
- Text: **The 6-Month Test**

## SCENE 3 — Rule 2: Buying is the beginning (2:05–3:35)

**VO:**
> Rule two: buying the right tool is the start of your responsibility, not the end.

**ON SCREEN:** Title card: **RULE 2 — BUYING IS THE BEGINNING** (tracker moves to ②)

**VO:**
> Most organisations pour their energy into choosing the supplier: security, data handling, contracts, features, performance.
> That work matters. But a great product can't fix a broken process.

**ON SCREEN:**
- Supplier checklist; all five items tick green.
- A shiny tool module slots into a tangled, knotted process pipeline, and the output still comes out messy.

**VO:**
> Once it's live, three things tend to happen.
> Managers use it in ways nobody planned.
> The data is solid in one part of the business and patchy in another.
> And a feature built for one small job slowly creeps into bigger, higher-stakes decisions.

**ON SCREEN:** Three quick vignettes, numbered:
1. A manager icon uses the tool sideways or off-label → "Unplanned use"
2. A map of the business with some regions green and some faded → "Uneven data"
3. A small "scheduling" feature arrow grows and stretches toward a "PROMOTION / DISMISSAL" box → "Scope creep"

**VO:**
> So don't just ask "does it work?" Ask the supplier:
> How do you test it?
> What data was it built on?
> How do you monitor performance?
> What changes when you update it?
> Can you explain an output when someone challenges it?
> And what support do we get if an unfair pattern shows up?

**ON SCREEN:**
- Big "Does it work?" crossed out.
- Six question cards deal onto the screen one by one in a 2×3 grid, timed to the VO.
- Header: **6 Questions to Ask Your Supplier** (a good freeze-frame or screenshot moment)

**VO:**
> Then get those answers into the contract.
> Promises made in a demo get very vague once there's a real problem.
> Be clear on who investigates, who fixes errors, and who tells you about changes.
> And keep the power to switch a feature off yourself, without waiting on the supplier's timetable.

**ON SCREEN:**
- A "demo" speech bubble full of ✨ deflates.
- A contract appears with three highlighted clauses: Investigate · Correct · Communicate
- A kill switch / off toggle moves from the supplier's side of the screen into the organisation's hand.

**VO:**
> You buy a system once.
> You govern it for as long as you use it.

**ON SCREEN:**
- Timeline: a single dot labelled **BUY**, then a long unbroken bar labelled **GOVERN** stretching off-screen.

## SCENE 4 — Rule 3: Watch what people actually do (3:35–5:10)

**VO:**
> Rule three: the real evidence shows up after launch.

**ON SCREEN:** Title card: **RULE 3 — WATCH WHAT PEOPLE ACTUALLY DO** (tracker moves to ③)

**VO:**
> Governance often stops at approval.
> The project passes its checks, gets signed off, and everyone moves on to the next proposal.
> But that's exactly when the most useful information starts to arrive.

**ON SCREEN:**
- Conveyor belt: project boxes get an **APPROVED** stamp and roll off-screen. The governance team turns its back.
- Behind them, data points start popping up around the approved box.

**VO:**
> Before launch, you test what you *expect* people to do.
> After launch, you see what they *actually* do.

**ON SCREEN:**
- Split screen. **EXPECTED:** one clean straight path. **ACTUAL:** many squiggly, branching paths.

**VO:**
> Managers might lean on recommendations far more than intended.
> Employees might avoid the tool altogether, use it for things nobody planned, or combine it with other information in ways that change the outcome.

**ON SCREEN:** The squiggly paths get labels as the VO mentions them: Over-reliance · Avoidance · Unplanned uses · Mixed with other data

**VO:**
> So monitor two things: the system, and the people using it.
> Is it producing accurate, useful outputs?
> Is anyone being affected differently?
> How often are managers overriding it?
> Are complaints, corrections or unusual outcomes building up?
> And has it spread beyond what it was approved for?

**ON SCREEN:**
- A monitoring dashboard with two panels: **SYSTEM** | **PEOPLE**
- Five gauges or widgets animate in: Accuracy · Fairness · Overrides · Complaints & corrections · Scope

**VO:**
> One warning: no complaints doesn't mean no problems.
> Candidates and employees may not even know AI was involved.
> A manager might blame themselves for a bad result instead of the tool.
> And some people simply assume the system can't be questioned.

**ON SCREEN:**
- An inbox counter reads **0 complaints ✓** in green.
- Zoom past it to reveal three greyed-out figures, each with a thought bubble:
  "Was AI involved?" · "Must be my mistake." · "You can't argue with the system."
- The green tick turns amber. Text slams in: **SILENCE ≠ SUCCESS**

## SCENE 5 — Recap & close (5:10–5:35)

**VO:**
> So, before the rules get written for you:
> Give every AI tool a named owner.
> Treat buying as the beginning, not the finish line.
> And watch what happens in practice, not just what was approved.
>
> Do that, and your rules shape how AI is used, instead of cleaning up after it.

**ON SCREEN:**
- The three pillars return to full size and each ticks ✓ as it's named.
- Callback: the rulebook from the cold open reopens, the **REACTIVE** stamp peels off, and a green **PROACTIVE** stamp lands.
- End card with channel branding and next-video slot.

## Notes for the motion designer

- **Style:** clean, flat, 2D. Icons and kinetic type, not stock footage. One accent colour per rule so viewers feel the progression.
- **On-screen text:** only key phrases (rule titles, checklist items, "SILENCE ≠ SUCCESS"), never full VO sentences.
- **Pacing:** a visual change at least every 3–5 seconds. Each list item should appear on the beat of its VO line.
- **Shareable frames:** "6 Questions to Ask Your Supplier" and the monitoring dashboard should each hold for 2+ seconds as clean, screenshot-able frames.
